Constitutional Maintenance Program — Family Architecture Atlas
Technical reference · November 2026 · Thirteen areas of governmental responsibility
This atlas explains the thirteen areas of governmental responsibility introduced in the Public Prospectus. It helps a reader locate a specific issue, identify related responsibilities, and distinguish possible failures from safeguards that are working. The short descriptions come first; later tables provide detailed research and source references for readers who need them.
The thirteen areas are an organizing map for research and possible legislative development. They are not findings that a constitutional failure has occurred, and they are not thirteen proposed laws. The accompanying Lean files verify specified properties of the formal model, not real-world legal or factual conclusions.
Reading guide: Sections 1–3 explain the thirteen areas and how they relate. Sections 4–5 are optional technical references: Section 4 indexes federal research topics, while Section 5 traces the map to the source record. Their letter-number codes are reference keys, not terms readers need to adopt. Sections 6–11 provide historical examples, standards for revising the map, requirements for developing inquiries, formal verification, and guidance for independent use.
The detailed source tables and historical records preserve the basis for the map. Readers may use, challenge, or adapt the method independently; they need not submit work to the original project or obtain central approval.
1. How the map works
The starting point is the responsibility at issue and what must happen for that responsibility to be fulfilled—not the name of an agency, the political topic, or a preferred legislative solution. A legal authorization does not by itself deliver a service; an oversight finding does not by itself correct a problem; and an official record does not by itself ensure that a successor institution carries out a continuing obligation. Each inquiry must establish which step matters, who is legally responsible, and what the evidence shows.
The map currently contains thirteen areas. Separate topics such as technology and revenue collection are addressed within these areas unless evidence shows that a genuinely distinct responsibility is missing. The twenty federal research topics in Section 4 help readers find a starting point; they are not additional constitutional functions. The source crosswalk and nine historical examples illustrate how assignments can be tested without treating any example as proof of a nationwide failure. No specific bill or constitutional amendment has been selected.
Three questions for placing an inquiry
1. What responsibility are we examining, and which institution is legally responsible for it?
2. What legal authority, resources, records, procedures, and evidence are necessary for that responsibility to be fulfilled?
3. What specifically would fail if those conditions were absent—and what existing safeguard or lawful result might show that the system is working?
2. Thirteen areas of governmental responsibility
Each area identifies a responsibility, the conditions worth checking, the failure that might be demonstrated, and neighboring responsibilities that must not be confused with it. The codes in parentheses are optional cross-references for the detailed tables. None of these descriptions establishes a particular historical failure or requires a new law.
Claims and correction (F01)
Responsibility: Give a person a lawful way to bring a claim or correction request to the institution responsible for considering it.
What to check: Person and claim identified; lawful entry; bounded classification if applicable; contest/correction route.
Possible breakdown: A claim or correction that should reach a responsible institution does not reach it or cannot be considered through the required process.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: Courts decide legal disputes; reliable information supports material decisions; records carry binding corrections forward; and service offices perform actual delivery. A lawful denial is not automatically a failure.
Representation and elections (F02)
Responsibility: Ensure that lawful public participation and certified election results reach the institutions authorized to act on them.
What to check: Participation/challenge route; canvass/certification; lawful interstate/federal handoff; operative authorization.
Possible breakdown: Valid certified consequence fails to reach its required successor, or an unauthorized actor takes the electoral power.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: State and federal authority, certification records, and later congressional procedures may each involve a different institution. A correctly completed certification is a functioning safeguard.
Congressional authority (F03)
Responsibility: Preserve the distinct powers and procedures of the House, Senate, committees, and Congress as a whole, including lawful approval and refusal.
What to check: Exact House/Senate power, threshold, proceeding and effect, with required records and legal transmission.
Possible breakdown: A required gate cannot operate or its result is unlawfully ascribed to another chamber/power despite surviving process.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: Congressional powers are distinct from appropriated resources, official records, and independent oversight. A lawful Senate decision is not a failure merely because the result was unwanted.
Public resources (F04)
Responsibility: Keep raising, spending, appropriation, allocation and legally usable public resources distinct from performed service.
What to check: Specific fiscal law and date; amount/purpose constraints; actual allocable balance and lawful recipient.
Possible breakdown: Legal funds cannot reach the responsible function, or expenditure bypasses a restriction, while adjacent institutions survive.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: The legal availability of funds is distinct from whether a program actually performs its work. Appropriation and spending questions require attention to the specific law, timing, and lawful alternatives.
Executive power and limits (F05)
Responsibility: Keep coercive and emergency executive actions within their lawful authorization, scope, duration, and review requirements.
What to check: Named action and affected persons; dated decision and permission; review and taper/inhibition where required.
Possible breakdown: An executive action begins or continues without the required authority or remains in effect after its lawful basis ends.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: A particular use of force is different from ordinary administration, external security authority, and the accuracy of the information used to justify an action. The evidence may support a lawful restraint rather than a failure.
Courts and remedies (F06)
Responsibility: Maintain competent adjudication and transfer a legally operative remedy to the correct implementing actor.
What to check: Jurisdiction; evidentiary/reasoned decision; enforceable order if any; bound recipient; verification of compliance where owed.
Possible breakdown: A court fails its assigned evaluation or an actual binding remedy cannot be lawfully carried despite competent surviving actors.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: Court access, judgment, transmission of an order, actual compliance, and independent verification are separate stages. An adverse but lawful judgment is not itself a failure.
Continuing public records (F07)
Responsibility: Preserve binding decisions and corrections so that later responsible officials or institutions can act on them as required.
What to check: Authenticated version; source and effective time; custodian; authorized next recipient; answerable later effect.
Possible breakdown: An operative decision or correction exists but is not transmitted to, recognized by, or applied by an institution legally bound to act on it.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: Evidence reliability is distinct from the legal effect of a record; issuing an order differs from carrying it forward; and replacing an office does not automatically end its obligations. Storing a record is not enough if a later actor must use it.
Reliable government information (F08)
Responsibility: Keep consequential public information appropriately sourced, independently checked when required, purpose-limited and correctable.
What to check: Source/incentive lineage; lawful purpose; risk-scaled corroboration; competent recipient; correction/contestation.
Possible breakdown: Information infrastructure functions, but an unwarranted or uncorrected assertion is accepted as reliable for a material decision.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: Technical system availability is not the same as the reliability of its information or the legal effect of an official record. Privacy limits and lawful confidentiality must be respected.
Independent oversight (F09)
Responsibility: Route independently warranted findings to accountable corrective actors and verify later capacity, where those links are required.
What to check: Applicable standard; inspectable population and modality; finding; recipient; dated remediation and retest.
Possible breakdown: A valid finding cannot reach responsible action or verification, even though inspection and operating offices survive.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: An oversight finding, the resources for a response, implementation of a correction, and later verification are distinguishable. Different inspection systems may have different statutory duties.
Program delivery (F10)
Responsibility: Convert lawful, funded programs and rules into real, accurate and timely decisions, services and infrastructure operation.
What to check: Dated program/authority; skilled carrier and systems; stage-specific measure; delivery; lawful review.
Possible breakdown: Authority and resources survive but staffed, technical or program performance fails at the actual public-consequence stage.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: Funding permission, evidentiary reliability, federal–state or Tribal authority, and institutional succession may each be separate questions. A functioning system is not deficient merely because a preferred program design differs.
Federalism and Tribal sovereignty (F11)
Responsibility: Respect the separate legal powers of federal, state, and Tribal governments while carrying out duties that require lawful cooperation.
What to check: Exact parties, treaty/statute/compact, legal time/territory, consultation/consent and final powers.
Possible breakdown: An owed crossing or answer fails, or unearned authority crosses a legal boundary, despite viable parties and process.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: Election administration, ordinary service delivery, international relations, and domestic sovereign powers must not be collapsed into one authority. Lawful consultation and cooperative arrangements can be functioning safeguards.
Foreign and security powers (F12)
Responsibility: Maintain lawful foreign, security, treaty, border, trade and external-effect interfaces under proper domestic constraints.
What to check: Named external subfunction; authorized domestic actor and instrument; receiving boundary; permitted refusal/action and review.
Possible breakdown: A legally required external action cannot be taken, or an action exceeds the domestic authority and constraints that govern it.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: Executive force, Senate advice and consent where applicable, and domestic federal–state–Tribal jurisdiction are separate legal questions. A lawful refusal or unexercised power can be an appropriate outcome.
Continuity and succession (F13)
Responsibility: Make lawful institutional change possible without losing obligations that remain in force or continuing powers that have expired.
What to check: Old carrier/legal termination; inventory of surviving claims and duties; lawful successor/OPEN disposition; transition evidence.
Possible breakdown: A right or duty that survives an institutional change has no authorized institution to carry it out, or a terminated power continues to be exercised.
Related responsibilities and lawful controls: Official records, funding, and routine program management must be distinguished from the legal duty to transfer surviving obligations. No general rule requires every office or program to continue indefinitely.
3. How the areas connect without double-counting
The same inquiry may involve several responsibilities at different stages. Those connections do not make the areas interchangeable and do not create additional legislative proposals.

	Assign each specific inquiry to the responsibility at which the relevant problem—or functioning safeguard—is actually located. If the evidence does not yet distinguish between two or more areas, record the alternatives and the facts needed to decide. Related responsibilities may be noted, but the same problem should not be counted as multiple findings. A single proposed mechanism may eventually address several independently established problems.
Certain questions apply across the entire map rather than belonging to one area: whether a proposed intervention has a lawful constitutional or statutory route; whether claimed measurements are valid for the population, period, and decision at issue; and whether an apparent local problem supports any broader institutional conclusion. Fiscal analysis is a later requirement for sufficiently developed proposals, not an estimate supplied by this atlas.

8. Requirements for developing an inquiry
The companion Inquiry Worksheet provides a reusable way to document a specific question: the responsible institution, relevant legal authority, affected people and time period, primary and contrary evidence, functioning safeguards, available remedies, implementation requirements, and any case for change. An inquiry may remain open, be resolved through existing means, or support further legislative development. No submission to the original project is required.
Work may progress from an initial lead through bounded evidence review, legal-authority analysis, and development of a specific mechanism. A separate, more demanding review is appropriate before anyone describes a legislative proposal as ready for use. A properly examined issue may also conclude that a safeguard worked, no failure was established, or no new law is needed.
If a change is warranted, the inquiry should compare lawful means already available, identify the authority required for any new mechanism, and examine implementation and fiscal consequences. Formal verification may test specified logical constraints, but it cannot establish factual premises or legal conclusions on its own. Naming an inquiry does not produce a bill.
